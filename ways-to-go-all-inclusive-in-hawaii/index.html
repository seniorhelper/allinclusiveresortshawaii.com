6 ways to get an all-inclusive-style Hawaii vacation

You can't book a Cancún-style all-inclusive in Hawaii, but you can get the same result: most costs prepaid, few surprise bills, little daily decision-making. Here's how, from closest-to-true to most flexible.

Updated November 10, 2026

The quick version: for the most "everything included" feel, take a 7-night inter-island cruise. For a land stay, pair a breakfast-included or resort-credit rate with a prepaid luau and one or two booked tours. For the lowest cost, rent a condo, do one big grocery run, and plan two or three dinners out.

1. An inter-island cruise (the closest thing to all-inclusive)

Norwegian Cruise Line's Pride of America sails 7-night round trips from Honolulu, with weekly departures. Itineraries typically call at Kahului on Maui, Hilo and Kona on the Big Island, and Nawiliwili on Kauai. Port order varies by sailing. You unpack once and wake up on a new island most days.

Usually included: your cabin, main dining rooms and buffet, basic drinks (water, coffee, tea), entertainment, and transport between islands.
Usually extra: alcohol and specialty coffee, specialty restaurants, Wi-Fi, shore excursions, and a daily service charge per guest. Drink and dining packages are sold separately and sometimes bundled as promotions.

Why this ship? US law (the Passenger Vessel Services Act) means foreign-flagged ships can't sail only between US ports. They have to stop at a foreign port too, so most other lines visit Hawaii on longer round trips from the West Coast with several sea days each way. Pride of America is US-flagged, so it can stay in the islands. Check itineraries and current inclusions on Norwegian's Pride of America page.

Trade-off: you get a day or so per island, not a full stay. Great for a sampler, less so if you want lazy beach days on one coast.

2. Breakfast-included and meal-plan rates

When you search a resort's own website, look past the cheapest rate. Many list a second or third option such as "breakfast for two daily" or "breakfast included." Some family resorts run kids-eat-free promotions in certain seasons. A true lunch-and-dinner meal plan is uncommon in Hawaii, but ask.

Is it worth it? Compare the rate difference per night with what breakfast costs at that resort's restaurant (the menus are usually online). If the gap is smaller than what two people would spend on breakfast, take it. If you plan to eat breakfast off-site most days, skip it.

3. Resort credit packages

Resort credits are a set dollar amount per stay or per night to spend on property: dining, spa, sometimes activities. They're common in luxury booking programs and seasonal promotions. Credits work like a partial meal plan, but read the fine print: credits are often non-cumulative, can't be used for room charges or taxes, and may exclude in-room dining or gratuities.

4. Flight + hotel vacation packages

Airline vacation brands, warehouse-club travel services, online travel agencies and Hawaii tour operators sell flight-plus-hotel bundles, sometimes with a rental car. These rarely include meals, so they aren't all-inclusive. They can lock in your biggest costs (airfare and room) in one payment, and bundles sometimes beat booking each piece separately. Always compare the package price with booking flight and hotel separately on the same dates, and check whether resort fees and taxes are included in the quote or due at check-in.

5. Condo + one big grocery run (the budget "all-inclusive")

This is how many repeat Hawaii visitors do it. Book a condo or suite with a full kitchen, land, and make one large grocery stop on the way from the airport. Oahu, Maui, Kauai and the Big Island all have big-box and warehouse stores near the main airports, and many grocers offer pickup orders you can place before you fly. Stock breakfasts, lunches, snacks and drinks for the week, then plan two or three special dinners out.

It isn't "free food," but it gets you the all-inclusive habit of not paying every time you're hungry, at a fraction of resort-restaurant prices. Many condo-hotels have no resort restaurant, so check what's within walking distance.

6. Prepay the big experiences

A large part of Hawaii spending is activities: snorkel cruises, helicopter tours, ziplines, luaus. Book the two or three you care about before you fly so the money is spent at home, not on the trip. Most commercial luaus bundle dinner (buffet or plated) with the show, and some include a set number of drinks or an open bar. Inclusions vary a lot, so compare. Book popular sunset cruises and luaus early in peak seasons.
