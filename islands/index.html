All-inclusive-style stays, island by island

No island has true all-inclusives, but some resort areas make it much easier to eat, relax and get around without constant spending decisions. Here's how each one works in practice.

Updated November 10, 2026

Quick pick: for the most self-contained resort feel, choose Ko Olina (Oahu) or Lanai. For easy, cheap eating without a car, Waikiki. For a classic beach-walk resort strip, Kaanapali or Wailea on Maui. For big resorts with lots on site, the Big Island's Kohala Coast. For lush scenery, accept that Kauai means a rental car and eating around.

Oahu

Waikiki: the "no plan needed" option

Waikiki is the opposite of an all-inclusive enclave, and that's its strength. Within a few blocks you'll find everything from fine dining to convenience-store breakfasts and plate lunches, so you never need a meal plan. Skip the rental car for the Waikiki days (hotel parking is expensive) and rent one only for the day you explore the island.

Ko Olina: the closest Oahu gets to a resort bubble

On Oahu's sunny west side, Ko Olina is a planned resort community built around four man-made lagoons with calm, protected water. That makes it a favorite with families. Its large resorts include a Disney resort, a Four Seasons, and timeshare-style villas with kitchens. There's little outside the resort area within walking distance, so most guests eat on property or at the small marketplace. That's the closest thing to all-inclusive living, and the reason a breakfast or resort-credit rate pays off here more than in Waikiki.

North Shore

One large resort anchors the North Shore at Turtle Bay, with surf towns, shrimp trucks and fruit stands a short drive away. It suits travelers who want to get out and eat local rather than stay on property.

Maui

Kaanapali

A long beach lined with resorts and joined by a paved beach walk, with a shopping and dining center in the middle. It's easy to vary meals between resort restaurants and casual spots without driving. West Maui is still rebuilding after the August 2023 Lahaina wildfire; Kaanapali's resorts are open, and visitors are asked to be considerate guests and support local businesses.

Wailea

South Maui's luxury strip: big resorts, a beach path linking several crescent beaches, and a shopping village with restaurants. Prices are high, so resort-credit packages are worth comparing here. The adults-only Hotel Wailea sits just above the coast. More casual and less expensive food is a short drive away in Kihei.

Kapalua

Quiet, green, and a bit removed, at Maui's northwest tip. Few restaurants exist beyond the resorts, which gives it a contained feel, but you'll want a car.

Hawaii Island (the Big Island)

Kohala Coast

The Kohala Coast, north of the Kona airport, is where the Big Island's big resorts sit, spread across old lava fields between white-sand coves. The resorts are large and self-contained, with pools, lagoons, golf and multiple restaurants each. Kona town and Waimea are a real drive away, so many guests end up eating mostly at the resorts, which makes credit or breakfast packages worth comparing. You'll still want a car to reach Hawaii Volcanoes National Park, a long day trip from this coast.

Kona

Kailua-Kona has more condos and mid-range hotels, plus plenty of restaurants and grocery stores. It's a strong base for the condo-plus-groceries approach.

Kauai

Kauai is the island where an all-inclusive approach makes the least sense. It's built for exploring, with Waimea Canyon, the Na Pali Coast and Hanalei, and the best meals are often from small local spots. Base yourself in sunny Poipu on the south shore, around Lihue and the east-side Coconut Coast for central access, or in Princeville on the lush north shore. A rental car is close to essential on Kauai.

Lanai

If you want the purest "everything happens at the resort" feeling, Lanai is it. The small island has one town, no traffic lights, and two Four Seasons properties: one by the beach at Manele Bay, and Sensei Lanai, an adults-oriented wellness retreat in the cooler uplands. Most meals happen at the resorts or in tiny Lanai City, so you're effectively living on property. Expect luxury pricing, and you'll reach Lanai by a short flight or the passenger ferry from West Maui. Check the ferry's current departure point and schedule, which have changed during West Maui's rebuilding.

Molokai

Molokai has no large resorts and is intentionally low-key. It's a wonderful place for a quiet, community-minded visit, but it isn't an all-inclusive-style destination, and we don't recommend treating it like one.
